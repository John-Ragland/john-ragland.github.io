Simultaneous source localization and ocean acoustic inversion


Since it's pioneering in the 80s and 90s, ocean acoustic tomography [@munk1995], has required that acoustic sources and receivers be localized with an accuracy down to meters. This accuracy has been required to be able to pin down the millisecond shifts in acoustic arrivals times that are attributed to temperature and salinity changes in the water column. Working with an ocean acoustic tomography experiment in the complex environment of the Gulf Stream extension and the New England Seamount chain, I have been able to use Bayesian inversion methods to simultaneously infer the acoustic source / receiver geometry and the ocean state posterior distribution.

With further development, this technology could enable robust self-localization (underwater GPS) while simultaneously providing an acoustic remote sensing update of the ocean temperature and salinity fields. Additionally, this method accurately captures the environment and geometry covariance.

A paper describing these results is in preparation and will be submitted to journals for publication this year (2026).

Acoustic fluctuations and coherence across the Gulf Stream


Fine scale stochastic oceanographic structure - like internal waves and spice - results in acoustic forward scattering, which makes acoustic phase and intensity stochastic as well. This creates a spatial and temporal limit to acoustic coherence which in turn has effects on acoustic based technologies like ocean acoustic remote sensing.

First, we extend existing WPRM (wave propagation through a random media) theory to the case of strong horizontal structure to accommodate the extreme environment of acoustic transmissions across the Gulf Stream front. We then present observed acoustic fluctuations statistics and compare them to the theoretical predictions assuming a power law internal wave field.

We show, in general, good agreement with theory and observation. However, we also highlight areas where the theoretical predicted statistics, like vertical coherence, are highly sensitive to small changes in the background sound speed field.
